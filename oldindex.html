When "Move Fast" Needs a Research Checkpoint
Joule Mobile · Feb 2025–Present
The Situation

SAP's AI-first strategy positioned voice as the key differentiator for Joule — their enterprise AI assistant serving 400K+ customers. By the time I joined the initiative, voice development was already underway. The problem: it had started without answering a foundational question.

Do enterprise users actually want to use voice for work tasks? And if so — which tasks, under which conditions?

Development was driven by executive enthusiasm and competitive pressure. Both are legitimate business drivers — but neither is a user need. I identified the gap and initiated the research independently.

The Critical Reframe
Original Brief
"How should we build Joule Voice?"
Assumes voice is always valuable. Leads to feature design.
My Reframe

Questions the premise. Leads to investment strategy.

Early Directional Findings
  • 01
    Voice is situational, not universal. The roadmap treated voice as a global input method. Research showed it's a mode that unlocks specific high-value moments — not a feature to add everywhere.
  • 02
    Hands-occupied, single-intent contexts win. Field technicians logging status updates. One clear intent, no visual complexity. Voice is a natural fit here.
  • 03
    Complex workflows break voice. Expense approvals, precision input, multi-step processes. Voice adds cognitive load — it doesn't remove it.
  • 04
    Cognitive load matters more than privacy. Expected privacy to be the primary barrier (per consumer voice literature). Enterprise users flagged cognitive load and workflow complexity instead. Recalibrated mid-study.
Strategic Impact So Far

Voice integrations for complex workflows paused pending end-user validation. Three previously siloed teams now operating from a shared criteria framework. Leadership framing shifted from "innovation signal" to "investment risk mitigation."

A Trust Problem Disguised as a UI Problem
Fiori Design System · Aug–Sep 2025
The Problem

Satisfaction had dropped to 3.71/5. Teams were building custom components instead of using the system — quietly, without filing tickets. The initial hypothesis was component quality.

I challenged it. Teams were avoiding working components. That behavioral signal told me this was a trust problem, not a quality problem — requiring a completely different approach.

Starting Assumption
"The components have quality issues — fix them."
Research Question
"Why do teams feel unsafe relying on this system — and on what dimensions?"

Three Root Causes
  • 01
    The Guidelines Conflict. SAP's internal guidelines contradicted Apple HIG and Android Material. Designers were caught between two authoritative sources — every decision required external validation, doubling design time.
  • 02
    The Detachment Loop. Rigid components → teams forced to customize → detach → lose future updates → build custom → debt compounds. Once detached, the cost of returning kept growing with every release.
  • 03
    The Accessibility Void. Developers couldn't verify WCAG compliance. Legal anxiety drove custom builds they "knew were safe" — even though those builds were objectively worse on accessibility. Fear of the unknown outweighed the known risk.

  • Screen-reader annotations integrated into every component spec — removing accessibility ambiguity

The "Stop Ship" Decision — Retry vs. Conversational Repair

Leadership requested a Retry button to handle AI errors for 440K+ users. I ran 8 customer interviews before engineering started — and found 8 out of 8 users repaired errors conversationally, never via button. The feature was deprioritized. The deck reached VP level.

Sometimes the Best Research Outcome Is Telling Your Team Not to Build Something
Joule AI Assistant · Sept 2025
The Situation

SAP leadership requested a Retry/Regenerate button to handle AI errors — a reasonable fix on the surface, serving 440,000+ enterprise customers. Engineering was ready to start.

I paused the pipeline. The assumption embedded in the request was that users think about AI errors transactionally — something mechanical that a button can fix. I hadn't seen evidence that enterprise users actually had that mental model.

Why I Rejected a Survey

A survey asking "would you use a Retry button?" would return false signal. People say yes to features in isolation. I needed to observe natural repair behavior — what users actually do when an AI response misses the mark, with no prompting.

I recruited enterprise SAP users who were also power users of mature AI tools (ChatGPT, Copilot) to benchmark whether enterprise context changes the mental model — or whether it transfers.

8 in-depth customer interviews
Thematic analysis + synthesis
The Core Finding

All 8 participants — when faced with a response that missed — immediately and instinctively rephrased their prompt. None looked for a button. Several couldn't distinguish "Retry" from "Regenerate" even when prompted.

"I don't know sometimes what exactly I'm looking for and [I am] figuring out... the journey along the way."
— Enterprise user, describing their AI error-repair process

Users experience AI error correction as collaborative exploration, not a mechanical transaction. A button solves the wrong problem — it actively misrepresents how users think about this interaction.

What Users Actually Wanted
  • 01
    Side-by-side comparisons of old vs. new responses — to understand what changed and why
  • 02
    Tone adjustment controls (more formal, shorter, simpler) — conversational, not mechanical
  • 03
    Transparency about why the regenerated response differs — trust through visibility
  • 04
    Interactive suggestions rather than a static button — dynamic, collaborative repair
Impact
  • Feature deprioritized — no longer treated as urgent
  • Team redirected to conversational repair patterns
  • Deck shared with VP of Research (unsolicited escalation)
  • Stakeholders: "validated concerns we already had"
  • "Conversational vs. UI" framework now used to vet future AI interface requests

From Design Deadlock to Merged Code in One Sprint
Joule Attachments · Oct 2025 · 3 weeks
The Design Deadlock

Engineering was blocked on the Attachments feature for Joule's AI assistant. The team was paralyzed by a design debate between two interaction models: Design A — Attachment Sheet (novel, auto-prompt capable) vs. Design B — Input Field (familiar, mirrors ChatGPT's "+" pattern).

Neither camp had data. A wrong decision would either confuse new users (high support cost) or frustrate power users (high friction). I stepped in as the impartial data partner to objectively settle the debate and unblock the backlog.

I deliberately sequenced quant before qual: UserZoom gave me rapid behavioral baselines at scale, then moderated interviews let me understand why the numbers looked the way they did. A/B preference testing quantified the directional signal from qual.

Unmoderated testing via UserZoom (n=47)
Moderated interviews + mental model analysis
The Strategic Trade-off

Quantitative performance was at parity — no statistically significant difference in Task Success or Time-on-Task. That meant the decision rested on cognitive load strategy, not performance metrics. Qualitative data had to drive the call.

"I mean ChatGPT operates the same way. It literally looks like this."
— Enterprise user, recognizing the Input Field "+" mental model

8/8 moderated participants immediately recognized the Input Field pattern from mature AI tools. Leveraging an existing mental model yields faster adoption than teaching a novel one — especially in enterprise contexts where users have limited tolerance for learning curve.

Recommendation: Design B (Input Field). In mobile AI, reducing cognitive load is paramount. The familiar pattern wins.

Phase 2 — Following the Finding

Selecting Design B was just the start. I initiated a follow-up study on the specific Attachment Cell Form component (Project 7) — the granular UI element users interact with after attaching a file.

Targeted usability testing revealed the "Status" state was ambiguous — users couldn't tell if a file was uploading, processing, or ready. I drove immediate UI refinements before the code merge. This ensured the right feature was also built right.

  • ✓
    Design B greenlit. Engineering merged code within the active sprint — no sprint carryover.
  • ✓
    Status state ambiguity caught pre-merge. UI refined before shipping — not discovered in production.
  • ✓
    UserZoom protocol established. Piloted as team standard for rapid unmoderated testing on future design decisions.
  • ✓
    UI Kit updated. Initiated formal Change Request; Attachment Cell Form component updated in the design system.

About

I think about where products break before they ship.

My background is in Human Factors and Cognitive Science — fields built on the premise that systems fail in predictable ways when designed without understanding how people actually behave under real conditions.

I bring that lens to enterprise AI. Before asking "how should we build this," I ask "what are we assuming about our users — and have we validated that?" That question has redirected roadmaps, deprioritized features, and prevented engineering investment from going in the wrong direction.
