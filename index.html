Context & Research Opportunity

SAP's help and support ecosystem had grown to 74 resources across 5 platforms — but self-service adoption continued to fail. When users hit problems, they either bypassed self-service entirely and contacted their Customer Success Manager (CSM), or attempted self-service once, encountered a poor experience, and never tried again. CSMs were absorbing the navigation burden the product was meant to carry. Before designing a solution, the team needed to understand the full failure landscape.

Research Program

Phase 1 — Ecosystem mapping (n=66, survey)

  • Catalogued 74 resources across 5 platforms and 6 resource types
  • Quantified the awareness-to-use gap: high awareness did not produce adoption
  • End users knew an average of 3 resources; admins averaged 7+
  • Established a behavioral baseline before any qualitative data collection

Phase 2 — User interviews (n=18, end users / admins / content creators)

  • 45-min semi-structured sessions: resolving a real problem, learning journey, and ideal future state
  • Identified 4 distinct entry triggers: error/blockage, onboarding, skill growth, content creation
  • Mapped two divergent support paths: direct-to-human (8/18) vs. self-service first (10/18)
  • 14 of 18 participants independently named AI as their near-ideal support model

Phase 3 — CSM interviews (n=11, independently led)

  • Explored how CSMs navigated and recommended resources and what customer behavior patterns they observed
  • Found CSMs were manually filtering 25+ resources to ~13, then customers selected 1–3 — a curation chain the product should own
  • CSM data corroborated Phase 2 from the outside, triangulating both user paths and friction points
  • All 11 CSMs independently named a role-filtered, single entry point as the critical missing capability
Key Findings
1
CSMs were acting as the navigation layer the product should provide
The manual curation work CSMs performed — filtering by product, role, and situation — was precisely what a well-designed panel should automate. Without that, customers either escalated immediately or experienced repeated self-service failures that built lasting negative associations with SAP support.
2
Self-service failure is cumulative — each failed attempt made the next less likely
Users described SAP support with words like "challenging, difficult, painful." CSMs reported customers gave up after one or two unhelpful results regardless of experience level. The failure wasn't a single bad interaction — it was a pattern that compounded across the customer relationship.
3
Four predictable friction points drove every failure
Discovery (no clear starting point), navigation (multiple windows, no clearer answer), contextual fit (content didn't reflect the user's specific setup), and terminology mismatch (customers searched in business language; SAP content used SAP language). These patterns held across all three phases and all participant types.
4
The problem was not lack of resources — it was lack of filtering
SAP had more than enough content. Even experienced CSMs actively recommended only about 13 of 25+ available resources. Adoption died in the gap between awareness and use — a discoverability and role-filtering problem, not a content volume problem.

"I'm like the Siri of SAP. They can ask me and I'll tell them where to go."

— CSM participant, describing their day-to-day role
From Research to Product Direction
Current state
User hits an issue → attempts self-service → fails → contacts CSM → CSM manually curates and filters resources → user gets an answer. CSM absorbs the navigation burden.
→
Design requirement (research-generated)
A role-filtered, AI-first single entry point that surfaces relevant resources by product, role, and situation — replacing the manual curation chain so CSMs can focus on strategic work.

The three-phase program didn't evaluate an existing direction — it produced one. All three data sets converged on the same requirement: 11/11 CSMs named it unprompted, 14/18 end users described AI as their near-ideal model, and the ecosystem survey quantified exactly where the filtration gap sat.

Cross-Functional Influence
The challenge
The finding that CSMs were acting as a manual navigation layer was a structural critique of the existing product. Framing it as an opportunity — "the panel should replicate what CSMs already do, at scale" — shifted the insight from a problem statement into a product brief that stakeholders could act on.
  • Used direct CSM voice as the anchor — "I'm the Siri of SAP" was more persuasive to leadership than any researcher summary
  • Visualized the curation chain (25+ resources → CSM curates 13 → admin selects a few → end user sees 1–3) to make the opportunity concrete
  • Cross-phase triangulation — three independent data sets converging — reduced the ability to dismiss any single finding
Impact
Next Gen Panel concept generated from research Role-filtered AI-first entry point defined as core requirement CSM escalation burden reframed as a product design problem Requirements validated across 95 participants
Reflection
Run the CSM phase earlier to sharpen Phase 2 recruitment
Phase 3 CSM interviews corroborated and sharpened the Phase 2 user findings — but running them sequentially meant I missed the opportunity to use CSM-observed customer behavior to target specific user profiles earlier. Running CSM interviews first, or in parallel with Phase 2, would have made the end-user sample more precise and the cross-phase triangulation tighter from the start.

02 · Joule AI Assistant · Sept 2025
8 out of 8 users never reached for a Retry button — so I recommended we don't build one
Leadership requested a Retry/Regenerate button for 440K+ enterprise users and engineering was ready to begin. Before building, I ran a behavioral study to observe how users actually responded to AI errors. Every participant repaired mistakes conversationally without looking for a control. The recommendation to deprioritize the feature reached VP level without being pushed.
Problem
The team assumed AI errors were transactional — fixable with a reset button. No behavioral evidence existed to validate or challenge that mental model.
My Role
Sole researcher on my first independent project. Scoped the study, recruited participants, facilitated all 8 sessions, synthesized findings, and presented to PM, Design, and AI leads.
Methods
n=8 behavioral observation interviews · think-aloud protocol · competitive benchmarking (ChatGPT, Copilot) · thematic analysis
Outcome
Retry feature deprioritized. Team redirected to conversational repair patterns. Findings escalated to VP unsolicited. A "Conversational vs. UI" framework adopted for future AI interface decisions.

Why Behavioral Observation — Not a Survey

A survey asking "would you use a Retry button?" would have returned misleading signal — people reliably say yes to features when asked about them in isolation. I needed to observe natural error-repair behavior without priming participants toward any specific control. Think-aloud behavioral observation was the only method that could surface the actual mental model users hold when an AI response misses the mark.

Key Findings
1
Users treated AI correction as collaborative dialogue, not mechanical repair
All 8 participants immediately rephrased their prompt when Joule missed the mark. None paused to look for a button or control. Users experienced correction as an iterative conversation — not a transaction that a reset could resolve.
2
The Retry vs. Regenerate distinction that mattered to engineers was invisible to users
Even when directly prompted to reflect on the difference, participants couldn't distinguish the two concepts during an active task. The label debate the team was having was solving a problem users didn't experience.
3
Users wanted transparency and steering controls — not a reset
Participants described wanting visibility into why a response changed, the ability to adjust tone, and side-by-side comparisons — features that match the conversational repair model they were already using naturally.

"I don't know sometimes what exactly I'm looking for. I'm figuring out the journey along the way."

— Enterprise user, describing AI error-repair behavior
Influencing a Skeptical Stakeholder Environment
The challenge
The feature request originated with leadership, and a direction was already implied. Recommending "don't build this" required a higher burden of proof than recommending how to shape a feature — behavioral evidence needed to be airtight and the framing needed to make the alternative clear.
  • Led with behavior, not interpretation — "8/8 users repaired conversationally" is harder to dismiss than a qualitative theme
  • Benchmarked against ChatGPT and Copilot to show the conversational repair pattern was not unique to Joule
  • Reframed the design question from "how should the button be labeled?" to "how should the system support users who are guiding it conversationally?" — giving the team a forward path, not just a stop signal
Product Implication

The "Conversational vs. UI" framework — distinguishing when natural language repair is sufficient from when UI controls add value — was adopted as a screening lens for future Joule interface decisions. One study created lasting methodology the team could apply independently.

Impact
Retry feature deprioritized before engineering began Team redirected to conversational repair design VP escalation — unsolicited "Conversational vs. UI" framework adopted for AI decisions
Reflection
Follow the disconfirmation with a validation phase
The study was rigorous at disconfirming the Retry button. It was weaker at validating the alternatives. A second phase testing tone controls and response comparison UIs would have turned "don't build X" into "build Y instead" — giving the team both a stop signal and a concrete direction to move toward.

03 · SAP Fiori Design System · Aug–Sep 2025
Designers were abandoning a working design system — the problem wasn't quality, it was trust
Satisfaction with SAP Fiori Mobile dropped to 3.71/5 and teams were quietly building custom components instead of using the ones available. The team's hypothesis pointed to component quality. Context & Hypothesis Challenge

600+ internal designers at SAP depended on Fiori Mobile. When satisfaction dropped and teams began building custom components, the first instinct was to audit component quality. But the behavioral signal told a different story: teams were bypassing components that worked correctly. That pattern — avoiding something functional — pointed to something structural rather than technical.

The Reframe
Team's hypothesis
"The components have quality issues — improve them and adoption returns."
→
My research question
"Why do designers feel unsafe depending on this system — and which dimensions of trust are breaking down?"
Key Findings
1
Conflicting design authorities doubled decision time
SAP guidelines contradicted Apple HIG and Material Design. Every component decision required external cross-referencing. Designers weren't avoiding low-quality components — they were avoiding a system that couldn't tell them what "correct" meant.
2
Rigid components created a debt spiral teams couldn't exit
Any customization required detaching from the component → detaching meant losing future updates → teams built custom alternatives → debt compounded with every release. The system's inflexibility was generating the abandonment it suffered from.
3
Accessibility anxiety produced worse accessibility outcomes
Developers couldn't verify WCAG compliance in the system, so they built custom components they believed were "safe" — even though those builds were objectively less accessible. Unverifiable risk produced worse outcomes than the risk itself.

"Sometimes I spend more time figuring out which component to use than actually designing."

— Internal designer, live workflow observation
Driving Consensus Across a Skeptical Team
The challenge
The team had already committed to a quality hypothesis. Shifting the frame to "investigate trust" required presenting the behavioral evidence — teams bypassing working components — as a signal strong enough to warrant a different investigation entirely.
  • Facilitated a cross-functional prioritization workshop — buy-in built through co-prioritization, not presentation
  • Interviewed designers and developers separately to surface role-specific trust failures before bringing groups together
  • Framed each root cause as structural rather than attributable to any individual, reducing defensiveness and enabling action
Changes Shipped in v25.04
  • Comparison tables documenting where SAP intentionally diverges from Apple and Android guidelines — with the rationale made explicit
  • Flexible component variants with clearly defined customization zones — enabling adaptation without forcing detachment
  • Screen reader annotations integrated into every component spec — removing accessibility ambiguity at the point of decision
Impact
+12.6% satisfaction in one quarter (3.71 → 4.18) ~8 weeks of engineering rework prevented 3 structural changes shipped in v25.04 2026 roadmap shaped by trust framework
Reflection
Use a diary study to capture abandonment decisions as they happen
Workflow observations captured a snapshot of behavior. A short diary study (1–2 weeks) would have revealed when teams made the detachment decision in real time rather than reconstructed after the fact — strengthening the causal chain and making the root cause harder to dispute.

04 · Joule Mobile AI · Oct 2025 · 3 weeks
Resolving a design deadlock with n=55 — and merging code before the sprint closed
Engineering was blocked by a design debate with no data: a novel Attachment Sheet versus a familiar Input Field pattern. I designed and ran a two-phase mixed-methods study in three weeks, delivered a clear recommendation grounded in user mental models, and unblocked code merge within the active sprint.
Problem
A data-free design debate was blocking engineering. Both options had legitimate advocates — and the wrong call would either confuse new users or frustrate experienced ones.
My Role
Lead researcher. Designed both study phases, ran quantitative and qualitative data collection, made the final recommendation, and independently initiated Phase 2 post-decision.

n=47 unmoderated usability (UserZoom) · n=8 moderated in-depth interviews · A/B preference testing · Phase 2 targeted component usability
Outcome
Input Field selected. Code merged within the sprint. A status state ambiguity issue caught pre-merge. UserZoom protocol adopted as team standard. UI Kit updated.

Why This Study Needed Two Phases

Both design options had advocates with genuine conviction and no data. A wrong decision carried real costs: confused new users increase support load; frustrated experienced users drive churn. The team needed an impartial data partner — not a tiebreaker with an opinion. Starting with quantitative behavioral data before introducing qualitative findings prevented any one camp from anchoring the conversation around early qual signal.

Research Design

Phase 1 — Concept evaluation

  • n=47 unmoderated (UserZoom): task success and time on task at scale
  • n=8 moderated IDIs: mental models and the reasoning behind the numbers
  • A/B preference testing for directional signal
  • Quantitative data presented first — gave both sides a shared behavioral baseline before qualitative interpretation entered

Phase 2 — Component validation (self-initiated)

  • Targeted usability testing on the Attachment Cell Form post-decision
  • Surfaced status state ambiguity: users couldn't distinguish uploading, processing, and ready states
  • UI refined before code merge — issue would not have been caught in production testing
  • Formal Change Request initiated; UI Kit updated accordingly
Key Findings
1
Quantitative parity moved the decision to cognitive load
No significant difference in task success or time on task between the two designs. With no performance advantage, the recommendation came down to which option imposed lower cognitive load on first use — making qualitative mental model data the deciding factor.
2
8/8 moderated users recognized the Input Field pattern from prior AI experience
Enterprise users had already internalized the interaction model from ChatGPT and Copilot. Leveraging an established mental model reduced adoption friction — a particularly high-value signal in enterprise contexts where training time and support capacity are constrained.
3
The status state ambiguity was only visible through follow-on component testing
Users couldn't determine whether a file was uploading, processing, or ready for use. The issue was invisible in the Phase 1 concept evaluation — it only surfaced through targeted component testing after the decision was made, demonstrating the value of following findings downstream past the initial decision point.

"I mean, ChatGPT operates the same way. It literally looks like this."

— Enterprise user, recognizing the Input Field pattern on first exposure
Navigating Competing Stakeholder Conviction
The challenge
Both design camps had strong advocates. Positioning myself as an impartial data partner — rather than an arbiter with a preference — was a deliberate strategic choice. Quantitative-first sequencing ensured that the behavioral baseline was established before qualitative interpretation entered the room, reducing the risk of advocacy-driven reading of the qual findings.
Impact
Input Field selected, code merged within sprint Zero post-merge bugs from attachment interaction Status ambiguity caught and resolved pre-merge UserZoom protocol adopted as team standard UI Kit updated via formal Change Request
Reflection
Run Phase 2 in parallel rather than sequentially
Running component validation after the design decision introduced a small but real delay. In a faster sprint cycle, that gap creates risk. Running Phase 2 as a parallel track would have surfaced the status ambiguity earlier and given the design team more runway before code freeze.

Context & Research Opportunity

SAP's AI-first strategy had generated executive momentum behind voice for Joule — serving 400K+ enterprise customers. The team moved from enthusiasm to engineering without pausing to answer a foundational question: do enterprise users actually want to use voice for work tasks, and when? Without that answer, three organizations were simultaneously building toward different, unvalidated user models.

The Reframe
Original brief
"How should we build Joule Voice?" — assumes voice is inherently valuable. Leads to feature design.
→
My reframe
"Where does voice earn its place in enterprise workflows?" — questions the premise. Leads to investment strategy.
Research Design

Phase 1 — Stakeholder alignment (n=7 PM interviews)

  • Semi-structured interviews with PM leads across AI, Voice, and Mobile orgs
  • JTBD framework: "When would an enterprise user hire voice over touch or keyboard?"
  • Cross-org assumption mapping to surface contradictions across teams
  • Thematic analysis and workflow mapping to identify where voice might earn adoption

Phase 2 — End-user validation (n=15 interviews, 7 job roles)

  • Contextual interviews in field scenarios to test the criteria framework
  • Hands-occupied simulation and screen-free workflow tasks
  • Voice validated in 2 of 7 roles: field technician and warehouse operator
  • Remaining 5 roles cited task switching cost and multi-intent complexity as disqualifying factors
Key Findings
1
Voice is situational, not universal — it earns its place in hands-occupied, single-intent moments
High value was limited to contexts where hands were occupied and the task was simple and singular — such as a field technician logging a status update. Complex, multi-step workflows added cognitive load rather than reducing it.
2
The top barrier was cognitive load, not privacy — consumer research didn't transfer
Existing literature predicted privacy concerns as the primary enterprise barrier to voice adoption. End users named cognitive load and workflow complexity instead. This mid-study divergence required recalibrating the criteria framework and reframing the product risk for stakeholders.
3
Three organizations were building toward three incompatible user models
AI, Voice, and Mobile leads each held a different mental model of who would use voice and why. Surfacing these contradictions through cross-org assumption mapping was the prerequisite for alignment — without it, each team would have optimized for a different user.
Influencing Without Authority
The challenge
Executive enthusiasm and competitive pressure were driving the roadmap — not user evidence. Intervening before the study was complete required framing the research as investment risk mitigation rather than a gatekeeping exercise.
  • Rewrote the brief independently — this was not an assigned question; I identified the gap and made the case to pursue it
  • Built a shared criteria framework adopted by all three org leads before end-user validation began
  • Shifted leadership framing from "voice as an innovation signal" to "voice as a targeted investment with defined fit criteria"
Impact
Speculative voice builds paused Three orgs aligned on shared adoption criteria Roadmap reframed from feature delivery to investment strategy Voice scoped to 2 validated high-fit job roles
Reflection
Run a cross-org assumption workshop before individual PM interviews
The contradictions between organizations emerged gradually across individual interviews. A structured assumption-mapping session with all three org leads at the outset would have surfaced conflicts faster, made the reframe easier to socialize, and reduced the time spent reconstructing a shared picture from separate conversations.
