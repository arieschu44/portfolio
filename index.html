Why This Research Mattered

SAP's AI-first strategy drove voice development for Joule — serving 400K+ enterprise customers. The team moved from executive enthusiasm straight to engineering, skipping a foundational question: do enterprise users actually want voice for work tasks, and when? Without that answer, three orgs were building toward different users simultaneously.

The Critical Reframe
Original brief
"How should we build Joule Voice?" — assumes voice is always valuable. Leads to feature design.
→
My reframe
"Where does voice earn its place in enterprise workflows?" — questions the premise. Leads to investment strategy.
Research Questions
  • Do enterprise users want to use voice for work tasks — and if so, which tasks and under what conditions?
  • When would a user "hire" voice over touch or keyboard input (JTBD lens)?
  • Where do AI, Voice, and Mobile org leads contradict each other on who the voice user is?
Methodology

Phase 1 — Foundational (complete)

  • n=7 PM stakeholder interviews across all SAP mobile apps
  • JTBD framework: "When would a user hire voice?"
  • Cross-org assumption mapping to surface contradictions
  • Thematic analysis + workflow mapping

Phase 2 — End-user Validation (complete)

  • n=15 end-user interviews across 7 job roles in field context scenarios
  • Contextual inquiry: hands-occupied simulation and screen-free workflow tasks
  • Criteria framework validated — voice earned adoption in 2 of 7 roles (field technician, warehouse operator)
  • Remaining 5 roles cited switching cost and multi-intent complexity as disqualifying
Key Insights
1
Voice is situational, not universal
High value only in hands occupied, single intent moments — like a field tech logging a status update. Complex multistep workflows add cognitive load instead of reducing it.
2
Cognitive load matters more than privacy
Consumer voice literature predicted privacy as the top barrier. Enterprise users named cognitive load and workflow complexity instead — requiring a midstudy recalibration that reframed the product risk.
3
Three orgs held contradictory user assumptions
AI, Voice, and Mobile leads each had a different mental model of who would use voice and why. Without surfacing these contradictions, features would have been optimized for three incompatible users at once.
Stakeholder Management
The challenge
Executive enthusiasm and competitive pressure were the driving forces — not user evidence. Challenging the brief without a completed study required framing the intervention as investment risk mitigation, not research gatekeeping.
  • Rewrote the brief independently — not assigned to question the roadmap
  • Built a shared criteria framework adopted by AI, Voice, and Mobile leads
  • Shifted leadership framing from "innovation signal" to "investment risk mitigation"
Impact
Speculative voice builds paused3 orgs aligned on shared criteriaRoadmap framing shifted to strategyVoice scoped to 2 high-fit job roles
What I'd Do Differently
Start with a cross-org assumption workshop, not individual PM interviews
The contradictions between orgs emerged gradually. A structured assumption mapping session with all three org leads at the outset would have surfaced conflicts faster and made the reframe easier to socialize.

Diagnosing a trust crisis disguised as a component quality problem

Lead Researcher. Challenged hypothesis, mixed-methods study, facilitated cross-functional workshop, drove v25.04 changes.

n=19 interviews · live workflow audits · pre/post survey · cross-functional workshop
Outcome
+12.6% satisfaction (3.71→4.18). ~8 weeks engineering saved. 3 changes shipped in v25.04. 2026 roadmap shaped.

Why This Research Mattered

600+ internal designers depended on SAP Fiori Mobile. When satisfaction dropped and teams began quietly building custom components, the instinct was to fix quality. But teams were bypassing components that worked fine, a behavioral signal that pointed to something structural, not technical.

The Critical Reframe
Team's hypothesis
"The components have quality issues — fix them and adoption returns."
→
My research question
"Why do teams feel unsafe relying on this system — and on which dimensions does trust break down?"
Key Insights
1
Conflicting authority sources doubled design time
SAP guidelines contradicted Apple HIG and Material Design. Every component decision required external validation. Teams weren't avoiding bad components — they were avoiding a system that couldn't tell them what "correct" meant.
2
Rigid components created a debt spiral teams couldn't exit
Customization required detachment → detachment lost future updates → teams built custom → debt compounded every release. The system's rigidity was manufacturing the abandonment it suffered from.
3
Legal anxiety about accessibility drove worse accessibility outcomes
Developers couldn't verify WCAG compliance, so they built custom "safe" components — even though those builds were objectively worse. Fear of the unknown outweighed the known risk.

"Sometimes I spend more time figuring out which component to use than actually designing."

— Internal designer, live workflow audit session
Stakeholder Management
The challenge
The team had already formed a strong quality hypothesis. Shifting them to "investigate trust" required presenting behavioral evidence — teams avoiding working components — as a signal that demanded a completely different research frame.
  • Facilitated cross functional workshop — buy in built through coprioritization, not presentation
  • Interviewed designers and developers separately to surface role specific trust failures
  • Framed each root cause as structural, not blame, assigning, to reduce defensiveness
Changes Shipped in v25.04
  • Comparison tables showing where SAP intentionally diverges from Apple/Android — and the documented rationale
  • Flexible component variants with documented customization zones — adapt without detaching
  • Screenreader annotations integrated into every component spec — removing accessibility ambiguity
Impact
+12.6% satisfaction in one quarter~8 weeks engineering rework prevented3 structural changes in v25.042026 roadmap shaped
What I'd Do Differently
Run a diary study to catch abandonment decisions in real time
Workflow audits captured a snapshot. A short diary study (1–2 weeks) would have revealed when teams made the detachment decision as it happened, not reconstructed after the fact, sharpening the causal chain.

03 · Joule AI Assistant · Sept 2025
8 out of 8 users never touched the Retry button — so we didn't ship one
Leadership requested a Retry/Regenerate button for 440K+ enterprise users. Engineering was staged to begin. I ran 8 behavioral interviews first, found every user repaired AI errors conversationally, and recommended deprioritizing. The deck reached VP without being pushed.
Problem
Leadership assumed AI errors were transactional — something a button could fix. No behavioral evidence supported that mental model.
My Role
Sole Researcher (first independent project). Designed study, recruited, ran all 8 sessions, synthesized, delivered to PM/Design/AI leads.
Methods
n=8 behavioral interviews · ChatGPT/Copilot benchmarking · natural repair observation · thematic analysis
Outcome
Feature deprioritized. Team redirected to conversational repair. VP escalation (unsolicited). Framework adopted for future AI interface decisions.

Why I Rejected a Survey

A survey asking "would you use a Retry button?" returns false signal — people say yes to features in isolation. I needed to observe natural repair behavior with no prompting: what users actually do when AI misses the mark. Behavioral observation was the only method that could surface the real mental model.

Key Insights
1
Enterprise users treat AI correction as collaborative exploration, not mechanical repair
All 8 participants immediately rephrased their prompt when AI missed. None looked for a button. Users experience correction as an iterative dialogue — not a transaction a reset can fix.
2
Users couldn't distinguish Retry from Regenerate — even when prompted
The conceptual distinction that seemed obvious to engineers was invisible to users midtask. The label debate was solving the wrong problem.
3
Users wanted transparency and control — not a reset button
Side by side response comparisons, tone adjustment controls, visibility into why a response changed — these matched the conversational repair model users already used naturally.

"I don't know sometimes what exactly I'm looking for, I'm figuring out the journey along the way."

— Enterprise user, describing AI error repair behavior
Stakeholder Management
The challenge
The request came from leadership with direction already implied. Recommending "don't build this" required behavioral evidence to be airtight — the burden of proof for stopping a feature is higher than the burden for shaping one.
  • Framed around behavior, not opinion — "8/8 repaired conversationally" is harder to dismiss
  • Benchmarked against ChatGPT and Copilot to show the pattern holds across AI contexts
  • VP escalation was unsolicited — triggered by stakeholders who shared the deck upward
Product Implication

This reframed the design challenge from "how should the button be labeled?" to "when should the system acknowledge that users are guiding it conversationally?" The "Conversational vs. UI" framework now screens future AI interface decisions.

Impact
Retry feature deprioritizedTeam redirected to conversational repairVP escalation (unsolicited)"Conversational vs. UI" framework adopted
What I'd Do Differently
Add a prototype phase to validate the alternative patterns
The study was strong at disconfirming the Retry button, but weaker at validating alternatives. A second phase testing tone controls and response comparison UIs would have turned "don't build X" into "build Y instead."

04 · Joule Mobile AI · Oct 2025 · 3 weeks
Breaking a design deadlock with n=55 — and merging code before the sprint closed
Engineering was blocked by a data free design debate: novel Attachment Sheet vs. familiar Input Field. I ran a mixed-methods triangulation study in three weeks and delivered a recommendation that merged code within the active sprint.
Problem
Design debate with no data blocked engineering. Wrong call would either confuse new users or frustrate power users.
My Role
Lead Researcher. Designed both study phases, ran quant + qual, made recommendation, initiated Phase 2 independently.
Methods
n=47 unmoderated (UserZoom) · n=8 moderated IDIs · A/B preference · Phase 2 targeted usability
Outcome
Design B greenlit. Code merged in sprint. Status ambiguity caught pre-merge. UserZoom protocol adopted as team standard.
55
Total participants

Engineering unblocked
0

Why This Research Mattered

Both design options had logical advocates and no data. A wrong call carried real costs: confused new users increase support load; frustrated power users increase churn. The team needed an impartial data partner — not a tiebreaker with an opinion.

Methodology

Phase 1 — Concept evaluation

  • n=47 unmoderated (UserZoom): task success + time on task at scale
  • n=8 moderated IDIs: mental models and the "why" behind the numbers
  • A/B preference: directional signal from qual findings
  • Quant first — gave both camps behavioral baselines before qual entered the conversation

Phase 2 — Component validation (self initiated)

  • Targeted usability on Attachment Cell Form postdecision
  • Identified Status state ambiguity: users couldn't tell if a file was uploading, processing, or ready
  • UI refined before code merge — not discovered in production
  • Formal Change Request initiated; UI Kit updated
Key Insights
1
Quantitative parity shifted the decision to cognitive load strategy
No significant difference in task success or time on task. Without a performance advantage, the decision rested on which design imposed lower cognitive burden — meaning qualitative data drove the recommendation.
2
8/8 moderated users recognized the Input Field pattern immediately
Enterprise users had already built a mental model from ChatGPT and Copilot. Leveraging an existing mental model reduces adoption friction — especially critical in enterprise where training time is constrained.
3
Status state ambiguity was invisible until Phase 2
Users couldn't tell whether a file was uploading, processing, or ready. This only emerged through targeted component testing — demonstrating the value of following a finding downstream past the decision point.

"I mean, ChatGPT operates the same way. It literally looks like this."

— Enterprise user, recognizing the Input Field pattern on first exposure
Stakeholder Management
The challenge
Both design camps had advocates with competing conviction. Positioning myself as an impartial data partner — not an arbiter with a preference — was a deliberate stakeholder choice. Quant-first sequencing reduced advocacy driven interpretation of qualitative findings.
Impact
Design B greenlit, code merged in sprint0 bugs from attachment interactionStatus ambiguity caught premergeUserZoom protocol adopted as team standardUI Kit updated via formal Change Request
What I'd Do Differently
Run Phase 2 in parallel — not sequentially
Running component validation after the design decision introduced a small delay. In a faster sprint, that gap creates risk. Running Phase 2 as a parallel track would have surfaced the Status ambiguity earlier and given designers more runway before code freeze.

05 · SAP Help & Support · 2025–2026 · 3 phases
The CSM was acting as the navigation layer the product should provide — a three-phase landscape study that generated the Next Gen Panel
SAP had 74 help and support resources across 5 platforms. No one — including experienced CSMs — could navigate them. I ran a three-phase landscape study across 95 participants that diagnosed why self-service consistently failed and defined the design requirements that became the Next Gen Panel.
Problem
Self-service failure pushed support load onto CSMs, who spent 60% of their time on reactive escalation work instead of strategic advisory. No one had mapped why this kept happening.
My Role
Researcher across all three phases. Designed and ran Phase 3 (CSM interviews) independently; contributed to synthesis and product framing across the full arc.
Methods
Ph.1: preliminary research + survey (n=66) · Ph.2: semi-structured interviews, end users/admins/content creators (n=18) · Ph.3: CSM interviews (n=11)
Outcome
Research directly generated the Next Gen Panel product concept: a role-filtered, AI-first single entry point that automates the curation work CSMs were doing manually.

74

Why This Research Mattered

SAP's help and support ecosystem had grown to 74 resources across 5 central platforms — but self-service adoption was failing. Customers who hit problems either bypassed self-service entirely and called their CSM, or tried once, got a bad result, and never tried again. CSMs were spending the majority of their time on reactive escalation and navigation work — the product was supposed to do this for them. The team needed to understand the full failure landscape before designing a solution.

Three-Phase Research Arc

Phase 1 — Ecosystem Mapping (n=66, survey)

  • Mapped 74 resources across 5 platforms and 6 resource types
  • Quantified the awareness vs. use gap: high awareness did not produce high adoption
  • End users averaged only 3 known resources; admins averaged 7+
  • Established baseline: where adoption was being lost before any interview data

Phase 2 — User Interviews (n=18, end users / admins / content creators)

  • Semi-structured 1:1 sessions: resolving a real problem, learning journey, ideal future state
  • Identified 4 distinct entry triggers: error/blockage, onboarding, skill growth, content creation
  • Mapped two divergent paths: direct-to-human (8/18) vs. self-service first (10/18)
  • 14 of 18 participants named AI as their near-ideal support model — unprompted

Phase 3 — CSM Interviews (n=11, my study)

  • Explored how CSMs navigate and recommend resources and what patterns they observe in customer behavior
  • Revealed that CSMs were manually doing what the product should automate: filtering 25+ resources down to ~13, then customers selecting 1–3
  • CSMs confirmed two customer paths and named the friction points driving each — corroborating Phase 2 from the outside
  • All 11 CSMs independently named a "one-stop shop" as the key missing product capability
Key Insights
1
CSMs were acting as the navigation layer the product should provide
The curation work CSMs did manually — filtering by product, role, and situation — was exactly what a well-designed panel should do automatically. Without it, customers either escalated immediately or failed self-service and built lasting negative associations with SAP support.
2
Self-service failure is cumulative and self-reinforcing
Each failed search attempt made the next attempt less likely. Users described self-service with words like "challenging, difficult, painful." CSMs reported customers gave up after one or two unhelpful results — and that pattern persisted regardless of experience level.
3
Four friction points drove every failure: discovery, navigation, contextual fit, search terminology
The same answer might live in SAP Notes, help.sap.com, SAP Community, or the support portal with no signal about where to look. Content used SAP terminology; customers searched in business language. Support content didn't reflect their specific product setup. One failed search and they stopped trying.
4
The gap between awareness and use was where adoption died — not lack of resources
SAP had more than enough resources. Even experienced CSMs actively recommended only about 13 of the 25+ available. High awareness did not translate to use. The problem was discoverability and contextual filtering — not content volume.

"I'm like the Siri of SAP. They can ask me and I'll tell them where to go."

— CSM 7, describing their actual day-to-day role
How the Research Generated the Product Direction
Current state (from research)
Customer hits an issue → tries self-service → fails → contacts CSM → CSM manually curates resources → customer gets an answer. CSM spends 60% of their time as a navigation middle layer.
→
Design requirement (generated)
A role-filtered, AI-first single entry point that surfaces relevant resources by product, role, and situation — automating what the CSM was doing manually, so the CSM can focus on strategic work.

The three-phase arc didn't evaluate a product direction — it produced one. The Next Gen Panel requirement emerged directly from the pattern across all three data sets: 11/11 CSMs named it independently, 14/18 end users described AI as the near-ideal model, and the ecosystem mapping quantified exactly where the filtration problem sat.

Stakeholder Management
The challenge
The insight that CSMs were acting as a manual navigation layer was a structural critique of the existing product. Framing it as an opportunity — "the panel should replicate what CSMs are already doing, at scale" — shifted the finding from a problem statement into a product brief.
  • Used CSM voice directly: "I'm the Siri of SAP" was more compelling to leadership than any researcher summary
  • Quantified the opportunity: 25+ resources → CSM curates 13 → admin selects a few → end user sees 1–3. The panel collapses this chain.
  • Corroborated findings across all three data sets — reduced the ability to dismiss any single phase as an outlier
Impact
Next Gen Panel concept generated from research Role-filtered AI-first entry point defined as core requirement CSM escalation load identified as product design failure Design requirements validated across 95 participants
What I'd Do Differently
Run the CSM phase earlier and use it to frame Phase 2 recruitment
Phase 3 CSM interviews corroborated and sharpened the Phase 2 findings — but running them sequentially meant I missed the opportunity to use CSM-observed customer behavior to target specific user profiles in Phase 2. Running CSM interviews first, or in parallel, would have made the end-user sample more precise and the cross-phase triangulation tighter.
