Research approach

How I work

Four principles that keep research from landing in a slide deck nobody acts on.

I work backwards from the decision

The brief rarely names the real risk. Before designing any study, I find out what would actually change product direction. If I can't name the decision, I don't run it.

Findings are framed as tradeoffs

I hand over a choice with the cost of each option made explicit, not a list of observations. A readout that doesn't change what the team does next didn't land.

Method choice is a hypothesis

I pick methods because they answer a specific question, not because they fit the timeline. Qual reveals mechanism; quant tests whether it holds. I sequence them so each phase narrows what the next needs to find.

Research that scales past the study

A study that answers one question once is the floor, not the goal. I build reusable assets: decision frameworks, screeners and synthesis templates that teams use independently on future work.

01 · SAP Fiori Mobile Design System · Aug–Sep 2025

A working design system nobody trusted.

I pushed for this study before I had data to justify it. 600 internal designers were quietly building custom alternatives instead of using the system. The team's read was a training problem. Mine was different: when that many people independently make the same workaround, the system has a structural failure, not a communication one. I challenged the framing before the roadmap was locked.

03 · Joule AI Assistant · Sept 2025

The button nobody reached for.

A VP requested a Retry/Regenerate button for 440K+ enterprise users. I pushed to run a behavioral study before engineering scoped a sprint. All 8 participants repaired AI errors conversationally without reaching for a reset control. The feature was deprioritized at VP level. A "Conversational vs. UI" framework now guides future AI interface decisions.

04 · SJSU Human Factors Lab · 2024–2025 · NSF-funded · Published AutomotiveUI '24

The driver no one had tested yet.

Every AV takeover system I studied assumed the driver could hear. Nobody had tested what happens when they cannot. I ran the study: 42 participants, 18 conditions each, a driving simulator and two years of NSF-funded lab work. The finding that surprised me most: no significant performance gap between hearing and non-hearing groups when the interface is designed right.

Background

I think about where products break before they ship.

My background is in Human Factors and Cognitive Science, built on the premise that systems fail predictably when designed without accounting for how people actually behave. At SAP I research AI assistants, enterprise tools and in-product support for both business users and end customers. I'm in the room when product decisions are still in motion: challenging assumptions before the study starts, shaping what gets scoped and translating findings into requirements that land on the roadmap. Research I run has stopped features from shipping, redirected sprint scope and set requirements that became roadmap commitments.

Methods: In-depth interviews · usability testing · contextual inquiry · log analysis · workshop facilitation · A/B testing · surveys · pre/post benchmarking · eye tracking · FDA usability (IEC 62366)
